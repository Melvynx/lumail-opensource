# Message Strategy and Research

Use this reference before drafting new copy, substantially repositioning an offer, writing for cold traffic, or producing variants intended for testing. Its job is to decide **what to say and why** before the writing process decides **how to say it**.

## 1. Build a source pack

Prefer evidence closest to the prospect and transaction:

1. Verbatim customer interviews, support messages, sales calls, survey answers, reviews, objections, and testimonials.
2. Observed behavior: search terms, click paths, purchases, cancellations, failed activations, and repeated questions.
3. Product truth: demonstration, capabilities, mechanism, limitations, delivery, price, guarantee, and service.
4. Proof: outcomes, examples, screenshots, numbers, comparisons, credentials, and third-party validation.
5. Competitive context: promises, mechanisms, alternatives, category conventions, and claims the market has heard repeatedly.
6. Frameworks and creative inference.

Do not average all customers into a vague persona. Segment by situation, desired outcome, awareness, prior attempts, and objection. Preserve exact phrases that carry emotion or specificity.

### Message-mining buckets

Sort source language into:

- **Trigger:** what happened immediately before they looked for help?
- **Pain:** what is difficult, costly, embarrassing, risky, or slow now?
- **Desired outcome:** what concrete progress do they want?
- **Prior attempt:** what have they tried and why did it disappoint?
- **Objection:** what prevents action today?
- **Decision criterion:** what must be true for them to choose?
- **Proof language:** what made existing customers believe?
- **Identity:** who are they trying to become or avoid becoming?

Promote repeated, emotionally charged, and purchase-adjacent language. Do not clean customer phrasing so aggressively that it loses recognition.

## 2. Choose the awareness level

Awareness is about what the prospect already knows, not where they sit in a generic funnel.

| Awareness | What the prospect knows | Opening job | Main argument |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unaware | Neither the problem nor the offer feels relevant | Enter through identity, story, symptom, or surprising observation | Reveal the problem without forcing a premature pitch |
| Problem aware | They feel the pain but do not know the solution category | Name and intensify the specific problem | Explain why it happens and introduce the solution category |
| Solution aware | They know approaches exist but not why yours is preferable | Lead with desired outcome or differentiated mechanism | Compare approaches and prove the mechanism |
| Product aware | They know the product but remain unconvinced | Address the dominant doubt or strongest proof | Clarify fit, value, proof, risk, and objections |
| Most aware | They know and want the product | Lead with offer, availability, bonus, deadline, or direct reminder | Make action easy and justify acting now |

Do not explain the category at length to a most-aware reader. Do not lead with product details to an unaware reader.

## 3. Diagnose market sophistication

Sophistication is how many similar promises and mechanisms the market has already heard.

1. **Fresh market:** make the direct desirable claim simply.
2. **Claim competition:** make the claim more specific, concrete, or demonstrable rather than merely louder.
3. **Promise fatigue:** introduce a distinct mechanism that explains why the result is possible.
4. **Mechanism competition:** deepen, prove, or differentiate the mechanism; explain why familiar approaches fail.
5. **Highly skeptical market:** lead through identification, experience, values, demonstration, or a new framing. Let proof and recognition carry more weight than superlatives.

Never escalate an unbelievable promise because competitors are loud. As sophistication rises, increase explanation and proof before claim intensity.

## 4. Create the message thesis

Complete this before drafting:

`For [specific situation], who want [existing desire] but believe/fear [dominant obstacle], [offer] is the [category or new frame] that produces [credible outcome] through [distinct mechanism], unlike [current alternative], as shown by [strongest proof].`

Then reduce it to:

- one audience;
- one desire;
- one required belief shift;
- one mechanism;
- one strongest proof;
- one action.

If the thesis needs several unrelated promises to feel valuable, the strategy is not ready.

## 5. Keep a claim-proof ledger

Before strengthening a claim, classify it:

| Claim type | Required support |
| --- | --- |
| Product fact | Product documentation or direct observation |
| Mechanism claim | Clear causal explanation, demonstration, or expert evidence |
| Outcome claim | Customer evidence, measured result, or explicitly framed possibility |
| Comparative claim | Fair comparison using equivalent criteria and current facts |
| Testimonial claim | Authentic attributable customer statement with relevant context |
| Urgency or scarcity | A real deadline, capacity, inventory, cohort, or operational reason |

Use one of four statuses:

- **Proven:** source supports the wording directly.
- **Supported:** evidence supports a narrower or qualified version.
- **Hypothesis:** strategically plausible but not customer-validated.
- **Missing:** requires a placeholder or user input.

Downgrade the wording when proof is weaker than the claim. Never upgrade the evidence in prose.

## 6. Diagnose conversion forces

Copy does not operate alone. Check five forces:

- **Motivation:** how strongly does this reader want progress now?
- **Value proposition:** why choose this offer instead of doing nothing or using an alternative?
- **Incentive:** is there a relevant extra reason to act now?
- **Friction:** what effort, complexity, time, money, or uncertainty does action require?
- **Anxiety:** what might the reader lose, expose, regret, or get wrong?

Do not compensate for weak value with manufactured urgency. Remove avoidable friction and answer anxiety near the action that creates it.

## 7. Design useful variants

Every variant must test a strategic hypothesis. Keep the offer and facts stable while changing one axis:

- desire: speed vs confidence;
- awareness: problem-led vs solution-led;
- mechanism: process explanation vs demonstration;
- proof: case study vs aggregate number;
- objection: time vs price;
- format: direct claim vs story-led entry.

For each variant, record:

`Variable -> hypothesis -> audience/traffic -> expected behavior -> metric`

Example:

`Opening angle -> problem-aware visitors recognize "tutorial hell" faster than a generic career promise -> cold YouTube traffic -> more hero-to-proof continuation -> CTA click-through rate.`

Do not declare a variant better before evidence exists. A polished sentence is still a hypothesis until the market responds.

## 8. Strategic preflight

Before moving to prose, verify:

- source language comes from the intended segment;
- awareness and sophistication are explicit;
- the message thesis contains one coherent argument;
- the dominant promise has a proof path;
- the mechanism is understandable and meaningfully distinct;
- the copy reduces friction or anxiety instead of only increasing pressure;
- proposed variants test decisions, not vocabulary.
